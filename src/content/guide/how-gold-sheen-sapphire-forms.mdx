The sheen in Gold Sheen Sapphire is not a surface treatment or a lucky accident of
light — it is a record of how the crystal cooled. This article explains the geology
in plain terms. It is a companion to the pillar, [what Gold Sheen Sapphire
is](/guide/what-is-gold-sheen-sapphire/).

<KeyTakeaways>
- The sheen comes from **exsolved iron–titanium oxides** — hematite, ilmenite and some magnetite.
- These grew as fine **platelets and needles** as the crystal cooled.
- They are **oriented along the basal pinacoid** with six-fold rotational symmetry.
- Light reflecting simultaneously off this aligned network produces the golden band.
- The process is entirely **natural**; the "flaw" is the phenomenon.

</KeyTakeaways>

## Corundum first, inclusions second

Gold Sheen Sapphire begins as ordinary corundum — crystalline aluminium oxide —
forming in aluminium-rich rock under heat and pressure. At the high temperatures of
formation, iron and titanium can sit dissolved within the growing crystal. The
sheen is written in what happens next, as the crystal cools.

## Exsolution: minerals separating out

As temperature falls, the dissolved iron and titanium can no longer stay in the
corundum structure. They **exsolve** — separate out — and crystallise as their own
tiny minerals inside the host: principally **hematite** (Fe₂O₃) and **ilmenite**
(FeTiO₃), often intergrown, together with some **magnetite**.[^1] Because the host
corundum has a definite atomic structure, these new minerals grow in preferred
directions, as flat platelets and slender needles rather than random blobs.

## Why the alignment matters: the basal pinacoid

The inclusions are not scattered at random. They lie along the corundum's **basal
pinacoid** — the plane perpendicular to the crystal's main axis — in a pattern with
**six-fold rotational symmetry**.[^1] This ordered arrangement is the whole point:
because the platelets share an orientation, light striking the polished dome reflects
off many of them at once, and those reflections combine into a single, bright band
rather than a diffuse haze. Tilt the stone and the band travels.

## From geology to gem

Two things had to happen together at this one Kenyan source: gem-quality corundum
had to form, and it had to carry a dense, well-ordered field of exsolved oxides.
That combination is rare, which is why the material is uncommon — and why the
[deposit's depletion](/guide/gold-sheen-sapphire-history-and-significance/) matters
so much. Understanding formation also underpins two practical questions: whether the
stone is [natural or treated](/guide/is-gold-sheen-sapphire-natural-or-treated/), and
how a cutter [orients the sheen](/guide/gold-sheen-sapphire-jewelry-and-settings/).
