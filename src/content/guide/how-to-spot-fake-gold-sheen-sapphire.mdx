Because its sheen is so striking, Gold Sheen Sapphire attracts imitation and
occasional misdescription. The good news: with a few checks and — for anything
significant — a laboratory report, you can buy with confidence. This article is a
spoke of the [buying guide](/guide/gold-sheen-sapphire-buying-guide/); use them
together.

<KeyTakeaways>
- Real sheen is **directional and moves**; imitations shimmer flat and static.
- Under a loupe, look for an **oriented platy inclusion field** — not gas bubbles or a join.
- Beware **closed-back settings, foil backing, and glued doublets**.
- Density/hardness anomalies are a red flag (leave testing to a professional).
- The decisive step is an **independent laboratory report** (natural, unheated).
</KeyTakeaways>

## 1. Watch how the sheen moves

Genuine Gold Sheen Sapphire shows a **directional sheen that travels** across the
dome as you tilt it. Warning signs:

- a shimmer that looks **flat and static**, the same from every angle;
- glitter that sits *on* the surface rather than gliding *within* it;
- a suspiciously uniform, "printed"-looking effect.

Real sheen behaves like light on moving water, not like glitter paint.

## 2. Look under magnification

With a 10× loupe, genuine material shows a **field of oriented, plate-like
inclusions** — the hematite and ilmenite that create the sheen (see [what Gold Sheen
Sapphire is](/guide/what-is-gold-sheen-sapphire/)). Look out instead for:

- **gas bubbles**, which point to glass;
- a **separation plane** or colour concentration at a join, which points to an
  assembled stone (doublet);
- a **mirror-bright foil** layer behind the stone.

## 3. Check the back and the mount

Imitations often hide their method at the back. Be cautious of:

- a **closed-back setting** that prevents you seeing through the stone;
- a **reflective foil** backing;
- a girdle that shows a **glued join**.

A seller of genuine material will generally let you examine the stone out of a
closed mount, or provide one that isn't closed-backed.

## 4. Weigh and measure

Glass and some substitutes differ from corundum in density and hardness. A stone
whose weight seems wrong for its size, or that shows easy scratching, warrants
scrutiny. (Hardness testing is best left to a professional — don't scratch-test a
stone you're considering buying.)

## 5. Get an independent report

No visual check beats a laboratory. For any meaningful purchase, insist on a report
from an **independent gemmological laboratory** confirming **natural corundum** and,
ideally, **no indication of heating**. Verify the report number with the issuing
lab. This is the decisive step — everything above just tells you whether it's worth
getting that far.

<Callout type="key" title="Bottom line">
A moving, directional sheen **plus** an oriented inclusion field **plus** an
independent report equals confidence. Miss the report on a high-value stone and
you're guessing.
</Callout>
