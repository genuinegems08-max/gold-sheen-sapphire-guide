"Rare" gets used loosely in the gem trade. For Gold Sheen Sapphire it has a precise,
verifiable meaning. This article sits under [History &
significance](/guide/gold-sheen-sapphire-history-and-significance/).

<KeyTakeaways>
- Rarity here is **structural, not marketing**: one source, now depleted.
- All known material came from a **single deposit in eastern Kenya**.
- **No significant new supply** is entering the market.
- Supply is effectively **fixed** — it can't grow when demand rises.
- Larger, high-sheen stones are the scarcest of all.
</KeyTakeaways>

## Rare by geography, then by depletion

Two facts make it uncommon. First, it was **never widespread**: unlike blue sapphire,
mined across many countries, Gold Sheen Sapphire came from **one deposit** in Kenya's
arid north-east. Second, that deposit is now **depleted** — the accessible material was
worked over a relatively short period and is no longer producing. The full account is
in [History & significance](/guide/gold-sheen-sapphire-history-and-significance/).

## What "fixed supply" means

For most commodities, higher demand eventually brings more production. That lever
doesn't exist here: no new mining means the population of cut and rough stones today is,
in effect, **all there will ever be**. Ordinary supply-and-demand can only redistribute
existing stones, not add to them.

<Callout type="key" title="The scarcity that matters most">
Fine, large stones with strong, even sheen are rare *within* an already-finite
material — a small subset of a fixed pool. That's the top of the rarity pyramid.
</Callout>

## What rarity does — and doesn't — mean for buyers

- It supports **long-term desirability** and makes **provenance** meaningful.
- It does **not** guarantee a price outcome — see the honest treatment in the
  [price guide](/guide/gold-sheen-sapphire-price-guide/).
- It makes **certification** worthwhile: confirm natural, unheated material before
  buying, per the [buying guide](/guide/gold-sheen-sapphire-buying-guide/).
